so:
- the top row is just "every integer lives in one of three buckets, based on its remainder when you divide by 3."
- the bottom row is the key bit: those are the only four ways to pick three bucket-labels (allowing repeats) so that they sum to a multiple of 3. either all three from the same bucket, or one from each.

so the whole problem reduces to this:

if i drop 5 balls into 3 buckets, can i always find either (a) 3 balls in one bucket, or (b) at least one ball in every bucket?

thinking about that — with only 5 balls and 3 buckets, is there any way to avoid both of those happening?
In fact writing up the argument properly so you can see the logic locked in:

proof. put the 5 integers into the 3 buckets by remainder mod 3. now consider two cases:
### case 1: some bucket contains at least 3 integers. pick any three of them. their bucket-labels sum to 0+0+0, 1+1+1, or 2+2+2 — all multiples of 3. ✓
### case 2: every bucket contains at most 2 integers. then no bucket can be empty — because if one bucket were empty, the other two would hold all 5 integers between them, which means one of those two would have ### at least 3, contradicting our assumption. so every bucket has at least one integer. pick one from each. their labels sum to 0+1+2 = 3. ✓
either case gives us three integers whose sum is divisible by 3. ∎
### the engine here is pigeonhole: 5 balls in 3 buckets forces something — either a bucket gets crowded (≥3), or the balls have to spread out to all three (≥1 each). there's literally no third option, and both options happen to win.
