# I Thought the NHS Was the World’s Second-Biggest Employer. Then I Checked.

*By Claudia G. Petersen*

I’d heard the line for years. The NHS, that great British institution, was the second-largest employer on the planet. Only Indian Railways was bigger. It had the ring of one of those pub-quiz facts you file away under “impressive but slightly useless.” 

So when I finally got curious enough to look it up properly, I expected confirmation. A tidy top two. Perhaps a little patriotic glow. What I found instead was a much livelier, messier story about how the world actually employs people in 2026 — and how a perfectly reasonable-sounding claim from the mid-2010s had quietly outlived its moment.

---

## Who on earth *is* Foxconn, and why are they so big?

If you’re anything like me, the first reaction is a tiny prickle of “should I be worried?” A company most of us have never encountered, sitting above the NHS and Indian Railways on a list of global employers? Is this some shadowy military subcontractor we’re all sleepwalking past?

Relax. Foxconn (properly Hon Hai Precision Industry, or Hon Hai Technology Group) is the world’s largest contract electronics manufacturer. They don’t design the iPhone. Apple does that. What Foxconn does is *build* them — along with iPads, MacBooks, PlayStations, Dell laptops, HP printers, and an astonishing amount of the world’s consumer electronics — in vast factory campuses, mostly in China but increasingly in India, Vietnam and elsewhere.

They are, in effect, the planet’s most important invisible assembly line. When you tap your phone screen, a significant chunk of the physical object in your hand was put together by people working for Foxconn. That is why their headcount reaches into the high hundreds of thousands to over a million at peak production periods. It is not mysterious. It is simply what happens when you are the company that actually makes the things the rest of the world designs and sells.

The “dark military subcontractor” vibe evaporates the moment you realise they’re the reason your new iPhone arrived on time. Slightly less Bond villain, considerably more “very large, very efficient factory operator.”

Defence organisations and state-owned giants still dominate raw numbers. But the private sector — Walmart’s vast retail and logistics machine, Amazon’s fulfilment centres, Foxconn’s factories, Tata’s diversified empire — is no longer a sideshow. The old lists from 2012–2015 often understated how quickly these commercial behemoths were scaling.

## The NHS and Indian Railways in their proper context

Both organisations remain enormous by any sensible standard. The NHS is still Europe’s largest employer and one of the biggest single healthcare systems anywhere. Indian Railways runs the fourth-largest rail network on Earth and moves billions of passengers a year. 

But when you place them next to Walmart and Amazon on the same chart, something becomes obvious.

**Figure 3: NHS and Indian Railways alongside two big private employers**

[Imagine the focused bar chart: Walmart tallest, then Amazon, then NHS, then Indian Railways.]

Walmart and Amazon achieve their scale with different operating models — heavy use of part-time and seasonal labour in retail, and aggressive automation plus sophisticated logistics in e-commerce. The NHS and Indian Railways are, by comparison, extremely labour-intensive essential-service operations. That is not a criticism; it is simply what the numbers reveal once you stop treating “biggest employer” as a single neat league table.

## What changed in the last fifteen years?

The old “NHS second, Indian Railways first” claim was never entirely accurate even in its heyday, but it was closer to the truth around 2012–2015. Since then the landscape has shifted in interesting ways.

**Figure 4: How employee numbers have moved since 2010**

[Imagine the time-series line chart: Amazon’s line shooting almost vertically upward after 2015, especially the 2020 spike; Walmart flatlining near the top; NHS climbing steadily; Indian Railways gently declining; Foxconn rising then easing back.]

Amazon’s trajectory is the most dramatic. From a relatively modest workforce in 2010 it has become one of the largest private employers on Earth. The pandemic years super-charged that growth as online shopping went from convenient to essential. The company added hundreds of thousands of people in a single year at one point — an expansion almost without precedent in corporate history.

Foxconn’s line wobbles. Production cycles cause peaks, and since around 2016 the company has invested heavily in automation. Some tasks that once required thousands of hands are now done by robots. The result is a workforce that is still huge but no longer growing in the same way.

Indian Railways shows the opposite pattern: fewer people on the books than fifteen years ago, yet dramatically higher passenger and freight volumes. Lower average labour costs in India make it economically sensible to keep more human roles in maintenance, operations and customer service. Automation is happening, but the calculus is different from a high-wage manufacturing environment.

The NHS line trends upward, driven by an ageing population, expanded services, and deliberate workforce growth plans. It is a story of rising demand meeting a labour-intensive model of care.

## The view through the dollar lens

Raw headcount only tells part of the story. If we ask a different question — how many employees does each organisation support per billion dollars of revenue or budget? — the picture changes again.

**Figure 5: Employees per billion dollars of economic activity**

[Imagine the horizontal bar chart: Indian Railways absurdly far out to the right; NHS also high; Foxconn and some state entities in the middle; Amazon and Walmart lower down.]

Indian Railways sits in a category of its own. Maintaining and operating a vast rail network with relatively low labour costs produces an extremely high ratio of people to money. The NHS is also labour-heavy by design — healthcare at this scale is people work. Amazon and Walmart sit lower because their revenue is enormous relative to headcount; technology and operational leverage let them move more economic value per employee.

This is not about “better” or “worse.” It is about different jobs in different economies with different cost structures. The chart simply makes those differences visible.

---

## So what was the original “fact” actually telling us?

It was a snapshot from a particular moment. In the early 2010s the biggest visible employers were still largely national champions — railways, health services, armies. Since then three things have happened at once:

- Platform and logistics companies scaled at unprecedented speed.
- Manufacturing giants quietly grew (or in Foxconn’s case, automated) in ways most consumers never see.
- Traditional public-service employers continued to grow or slowly rationalise according to their own economics and politics.

The neat top-two story was always more folklore than settled fact. The real list is messier, more global, and more revealing about how different parts of the economy actually employ people.

---

## The small pleasure of looking things up

I started this because I wanted to know whether the NHS really was the silver medallist on the world employment podium. It isn’t, quite. But the journey turned out to be more interesting than the destination.

Indian Railways remains an astonishing human enterprise — fewer staff than it once had, moving far more people and goods. The NHS is still a colossal, labour-intensive organisation that most countries would struggle to replicate. Foxconn turns out to be the company that literally builds a surprising amount of the technology we all carry around. Amazon went from niche bookseller to one of the largest private workforces on Earth in barely two decades. And the biggest employers by raw numbers are still, in several cases, national defence establishments.

None of that is particularly shocking once you see the charts. But it is satisfying to have the picture in focus instead of the blurry, slightly out-of-date version that had been floating around.

Sometimes the most useful thing you can do with a pub-quiz fact is to treat it as a starting point rather than an ending. The world kept moving while the story stayed still. Checking the numbers is how you catch up.
