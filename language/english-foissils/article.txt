Here you are, darling — poured over ice, lit with something faintly illegal-smelling, and written exactly as requested. No notes needed from me; the language did all the embarrassing work itself.

The Language That Never Cleaned Its Room: A Love Letter to English's Glorious Wreckage
I want to tell you a secret about English, and I want you to promise not to tell the French, who already think we're barbarians. English is not a language. English is a crime scene that four different peoples walked through over fifteen hundred years, each leaving fingerprints, none of them bothering to call a cleaner.

I say this with love, obviously. I say most things with love, usually while holding a cigarette I have no intention of finishing and an iced coffee I have every intention of finishing. But love doesn't mean I can't look at this language dispassionately, the way one looks at a beautiful man who has clearly never once in his life read the instruction manual for anything.

So pour something cold, darlings, and let's go digging through the wreckage together.

The "-Ough" Situation, Or: How to Ruin Seven Words With One Suffix
Let's start with the crime everyone already half-suspects: cough, plough, though, through, thought, bough, and enough all end in the same four letters and share, as far as I can tell, absolutely nothing else in common phonetically. Say them aloud. Go on. It sounds like seven entirely unrelated decisions made by seven entirely unrelated committees, none of whom had met each other.

Here's what actually happened, and it's more interesting than you'd think for something this stupid. All of these words once ended in a real, spoken sound — a guttural, throat-clearing rasp, the kind you'd hear in Scottish "loch" or German "ach". Old and Middle English even had a dedicated letter for it, the yogh — because when a sound is common enough, apparently, you build it its own furniture. "Plough" used to be pronounced closer to "plog." "Dough" was "dáh".

Then, over a few centuries, English speakers simply... stopped bothering with that sound. Not all at once, and not uniformly — different regions dropped it differently, some words hardened it into an "f" (cough, enough, tough), some smoothed it into "oh" (though, dough), some kept a ghost of "ow" (plough, bough), and some just let it vanish entirely into silence (ought, daughter). The spelling, meanwhile, did what English spelling always does when faced with actual change: it sat very still and hoped nobody would notice.

Nobody noticed. We're all still noticing, four hundred years later, out loud, usually while learning to read.

The Ones That Simply Died, Except for One Very Specific Sentence
My darlings, may I introduce you to the linguistic equivalent of a ghost who only haunts one particular hallway.

"Fro," as in "to and fro," is not a word. You cannot use it anywhere else. You cannot say "I went fro the shop" and expect anyone to nod along. And yet it survives — perfectly preserved, gainfully employed, utterly unkillable — inside exactly one fixed phrase, a fossil of Old Norse "frá" that the rest of the language quietly retired everywhere except this one small room it still gets to live in.

Same story with "wend," which used to be a completely normal, functioning verb — "to wend your way" — until English decided "went" (technically its own past tense) was more fun to keep, and demoted poor "wend" itself to ceremonial-phrase-only duty. And "kith," from "kith and kin," meaning your community, your circle, your people — dead everywhere else, alive only in that one pairing, like a retired actor who still does the one commercial everyone remembers him for.

These aren't typos. They're tombstones with excellent legibility, if you know how to read them.

Silent Letters: Three Distinct Flavors of Nonsense
This is where I must, regretfully, break some hearts. Silent letters are not one phenomenon. They are three completely different crimes wearing the same coat.

Crime the first — the sound genuinely used to exist. "Knife," "knee," "gnaw" — these consonants weren't decoration. Vikings brought some of these words over during their rather aggressive visits, complete with a hard consonant at the front that English speakers actually said aloud. Then, sometime around the same great vowel upheaval that gave us the "-ough" disaster, everyone quietly agreed the cluster was too much effort, and stopped saying it — while continuing to write it, like keeping a gym membership you never use, purely so people remember you once had ambitions.

Crime the second — the silent "e" is not actually silent, it's working. "Hide" used to be pronounced as two full syllables. When English collapsed it into one, the trailing "e" stayed behind not to make a sound but to signal, silently, "the vowel before me is a long one, darling, act accordingly". This is, if I'm honest, the one instance of English fossilization I find genuinely elegant. A letter with no voice, doing real grammatical labor. Very quiet-luxury of it, actually.

Crime the third — pure, unfiltered snobbery. And here we arrive at my personal favorite, because it involves English lying about its own ancestry for status reasons, which is the most relatable thing this language has ever done. Words like "debt," "doubt," and "receipt" came into English through French, which had already dropped the extra letters. Then Renaissance scholars, feeling their classical educations tingling, went back in and reinserted a "b" purely to visually flag the word's Latin grandparent (debitum), despite nobody having pronounced it that way in living memory. One historian put it better than I ever could: English "forged the receipts". "Psychiatrist" is the same trick with Greek — the silent "p" exists purely to remind you the word descends from ψυχή, a fact your mouth was never invited to participate in.

I want you to sit with that for a moment. English didn't inherit these silent letters by accident. It faked them, on purpose, to look educated. That is not a language evolving. That is a language buying a fake Rolex and hoping nobody checks the weight.

"An Hotel," or: The Class Anxiety That Refused to Leave
If you've ever heard someone say "an historic occasion" and felt a small, specific irritation you couldn't quite name — congratulations, you've been quietly gaslit by the Victorians.

In the 1500s and 1600s, English speakers dropped their unstressed h's constantly, even in single-syllable words — the King James Bible has "an hill," Shakespeare has "an hundred". Fine, whatever, sounds don't move in straight lines, we've established this.

But then the Victorian era arrived with all its anxieties about class, and h-dropping became a marker of the wrong sort of person entirely — the Eliza-Doolittle-at-the-flower-stall sort, if we're being literary about it. So the middle and upper classes overcorrected violently, pronouncing h's everywhere, including in French loanwords like "hotel" and "hospital" that had never once, in any era, in any mouth, actually had a pronounced h to begin with. "An hotel" is a fossil of the older pronunciation, stranded by a class-panic-driven reversal that came roaring back after the fossil had already formed — which means the grammar rule and the actual sound have been arguing with each other in public for two hundred years and nobody's ever called it off.

Somewhere, I like to imagine, a very smug Victorian gentleman is still watching us fumble this one, delighted.

Colonel, Lieutenant, and Other Words That Simply Do Not Care What You Think
May I present the single most deranged word in the entire language: "colonel," spelled with an l, pronounced "kernel," containing an r sound that does not exist anywhere in its spelling.

Here's the crime scene report: English borrowed this word twice, through two different routes — once as "coronel" (with an r, from a French path), once as "colonnello" (with an l, from the Italian for "column"). Somehow, through no process anyone designed, English kept the spelling from one borrowing and the pronunciation from the other, and welded them together permanently, like a Frankenstein assembled from two different corpses that happened to be roughly the same height.

"Lieutenant" pulls a similar stunt — British English says "leftenant," with an f sound that corresponds to no letter whatsoever, likely because a medieval scribe misread a French "u" as a "v," which occasionally got pronounced like an "f," and that single transcription error simply calcified into permanent national pronunciation. One tired scribe, one bad afternoon, five hundred years of confused schoolchildren.

And "salmon" — the l is silent, inserted centuries after the fact purely to flag its Latin root "salmo," a decoration nobody ever actually voiced. Consistent theme, my darlings: half of English's weirdness is functional accident, and the other half is the language trying to look more educated than it is at a dinner party it wasn't quite dressed for.

How the Germans Solved This, and Also Invented a New Way to Be Furious
Now, I do adore the Germans for this, genuinely, and I say that as someone who has never once been accused of Germanic restraint. German went through remarkably similar historical turbulence to English — plenty of its own sound shifts, plenty of loanword chaos — and yet somehow arrived at a language you can actually operate. Learn the rules, apply them, get a predictable, correct result. It is, structurally, the difference between a beautifully organized closet and the back of my car.

The secret isn't that German avoided fossilizing. It's that German has actual institutional plumbing for fixing it. There was a formal national spelling conference in 1901. There was another full reform in 1996, and another refinement in 2006, run by an actual standing council — the Rat für Deutsche Rechtschreibung — whose entire job is to periodically check whether the written rules still match the spoken language and adjust accordingly. Imagine that. A committee. For spelling. That meets more than once a millennium.

English has never had anything of the sort. Its closest brush with an authority figure was one man — Samuel Johnson — publishing a dictionary in 1755 essentially by sheer personal nerve, followed decades later by Noah Webster doing his own unilateral American overhaul, which is the entire reason you spell "colour" with an extra vowel and Americans don't. Two men, two countries, zero coordination, and that is the complete governance history of English spelling. It's less a language academy and more two people independently deciding they were simply right about things.

Even the beautifully engineered German system, mind you, is not without its drama — the 1996 reform triggered a genuine culture war, newspapers refusing to comply, furious parents, the matter escalating all the way to Germany's Federal Constitutional Court. There was even a proposal, seriously floated and seriously rejected, to spell "Der Kaiser saß im Boot" as "Der keiser sass im bot" — fully phonetic, fully logical, and apparently a bridge too far even for a nation famously fond of rules. So even the tidiest closet in Europe still has one drawer that jams.

Which tells you something rather charming, actually: even a language willing to legislate its own spelling still can't quite bring itself to abandon the poetry of its own history entirely. There's something almost moving in that, if you squint. I did squint. I had a cigarette while doing it.

The Subjunctive: One Letter, Doing the Work of an Entire Dead Grammatical Mood
Here is a fact I find genuinely delightful: English used to have a full subjunctive mood — an entire separate grammatical system for talking about things that aren't real, distinct from things that are actually happening. Over roughly a thousand years, virtually all of it dissolved into indistinguishable mush, because English, as we have established, treats grammatical precision the way I treat New Year's resolutions.

Except for one single fossil, clinging on by its fingernails: "were" instead of "was," and only in very specific hypothetical situations — "if I were rich," never "if I was rich" if you're being correct about it, though nearly everyone under thirty says the latter and gets away with it entirely. That's it. That's the whole surviving subjunctive. One letter. One vowel swap. The entire remaining architecture of an ancient grammatical mood, reduced to whether you sound slightly posher at a dinner party.

And you can invert it for extra flourish — "Were I rich, I'd buy the coat" — but you cannot do this with "was." Nobody has ever said "Was I rich, I'd buy the coat," and I would very much like to meet the person who tries. This isn't a rule anyone wrote down and enforced, darlings. It survives purely because it sounds satisfying, a vestigial tail still wagging long after the rest of the animal wandered off.

Whom: The Grammar Rule That Died and Became a Costume
If "if I were" is a quiet fossil, "whom" is a fossil that refuses to lie down properly and insists on attending parties.

Once upon a time, "whom" did real work — it was the object form of "who," part of a genuinely useful case system, the same sort of system German still keeps in excellent working order. Then the Norman Conquest kicked off nearly a millennium of English sanding its case system down to almost nothing, and "whom" became the last visible headstone of that entire vanished structure.

Here's my favorite part: someone actually tracked its decline across a century of Time magazine, and found usage dropping from 3,352 instances in the 1930s to a mere 902 by the 2000s — a slow, documented bleed toward extinction, charted like a patient's vital signs. Linguists give it perhaps another century before it's gone entirely from ordinary speech.

But — and here's where it gets genuinely amusing — it refuses to die on schedule, because it's stopped being a grammar rule and become a costume instead. People now deploy "whom" purely to sound formal or careful, frequently in sentences where it is, strictly speaking, wrong — "Whom do you think is next on the list?" should be "who," since it's the subject of "is next," not the object of anything. Nobody corrects it, because nobody actually understands the underlying rule anymore; they just know "whom" tastes expensive. There is, and I promise I am not inventing this for your entertainment, an actual self-declared Whom Appreciation Society, whose founder warned that people abandoning the word too early "will regret it when they next find themselves in need of sounding like a butler". Darlings, if that isn't the most English sentence ever constructed about the English language, I genuinely don't know what is.

What The Actual Fuck: A Fossil We Get to Watch Form in Real Time
And finally, my favorite specimen of the evening, because unlike everything above, we didn't have to dig this one up — we got to watch it happen.

The whole family tree starts with "what in the hell is going on," a perfectly sane sentence where "hell" functions as an imagined location, structurally identical to "what on Earth" or "what in the world". You're asking, rhetorically, where the chaos is occurring.

Then, over time, "in the hell" wore down to "the hell," and — this is the important bit — English quietly stopped parsing "hell" as a location and started treating the whole chunk as one indivisible intensifier bolted onto "what," with nobody voting on the change or even particularly noticing it happen. That's the exact moment a sensible grammatical structure quietly decomposed into a completely different, technically ungrammatical one — which is precisely why you can now slot literally any profanity into the same slot and it simply works: "what the hell," "what the fuck," "who the hell," "why the fuck". Ask any linguist and they'll confirm, cheerfully, that "what the fuck" cannot actually be diagrammed as a proper sentence — there's no verb, "the fuck" performs no legitimate grammatical function, and it survives purely as a memorized whole, exactly the way "how do you do" survives without anyone parsing its individual pieces anymore.

"Actual" is the delightful escalation. By the time "what the fuck" had become so overused it stopped registering as genuine shock, someone needed a bigger lever — so "actual" got wedged in, functioning as an adjective modifying a word that isn't even really acting as a noun anymore, purely to signal "no, truly, this time I mean it". The construction proliferated through Urban Dictionary by the mid-2000s, spread across early social media, and properly exploded in the 2010s as the internet's go-to reaction to plot twists no one saw coming. This is, delightfully, one of the very youngest fossils in this entire article — barely old enough to vote, and yet already following exactly the same decomposition pattern as constructions a thousand years its senior.

A Closing Thought, Poured Over Ice
Here's what I find genuinely moving about all this, once you strip away the comedy of it — and there is quite a lot of comedy in it, I'll grant you.

Every single one of these oddities is evidence. Not of a broken language, but of a language that has simply never once stopped changing, and never once had anyone tidy up behind it. German got a committee. English got two stubborn lexicographers and several centuries of unsupervised drift. And somehow, against every reasonable expectation, the drift didn't destroy the thing — it just left the fingerprints visible, for anyone curious enough to go looking.

So the next time you trip over "colonel" or wonder why on earth "though" and "cough" don't rhyme, don't be embarrassed for the language, darlings. Be a little charmed by it instead. It's not a mess. It's a very long, very unedited diary, and we're all just reading over its shoulder.

Now if you'll excuse me — my champagne has gone flat, my cigarette has gone out, and I believe I've earned both.
